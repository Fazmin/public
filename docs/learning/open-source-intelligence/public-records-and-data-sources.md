# Public Records and Data Sources

## Description

Public Records and Data Sources encompass a wide range of openly available documents and structured databases, including government filings, court records, corporate registries, patent databases, and regulatory disclosures. Analysts leverage these sources to uncover patterns, ownership trails, infrastructure details, and affiliations that may not be immediately obvious. While often appearing routine, this information when analyzed systematically can reveal critical insights relevant to investigations, compliance, and security assessments.

## Why This Matters
Organizations rely on public records and data sources for regulatory compliance, reporting, and institutional planning. Accessing and analyzing these sources helps operational teams:
- Verify institutional, departmental, or research-related filings and disclosures. Accurate review ensures compliance with government and funding requirements.
- Identify potential risks or gaps in administrative or infrastructure oversight. Publicly available data may reveal overlooked connections or vulnerabilities.
- Support strategic decision-making through demographic, geographic, or sector-specific data. This helps guide planning, procurement, and partnership decisions.
- Staff can validate compliance filings, review contracts, and ensure transparency in operations.
- Researchers can access open datasets, regulatory filings, and historical records to enhance analyses.
- Students can utilize public data for projects, case studies, or investigative assignments, while learning proper data validation and ethical use.

Without careful attention to public records, organizations can risk missing critical insights, potentially impacting compliance, operational efficiency, or reputation.

## Skill Development

- Research: How can structured data in public records support attribution and link analysis in open-source investigations?
- Investigate: What methods can be used to identify relevant public databases and efficiently extract target-specific information?
- Research: How do analysts validate and cross-reference findings from public records to ensure accuracy and context?
- Reflect: In what ways can the misuse or oversight of public disclosures lead to unintended information exposure?
- Reflect: How does the analysis of public records contribute to identifying organizational structure or supply chain relationships?
- Investigate: Public records vary by jurisdiction in what they contain and how they are accessed. How would you locate the right registry or records system for a target in a country or region you are unfamiliar with, and what are the limits of combining records across jurisdictions?
- Reflect: Aggregating many individually harmless public records can reveal sensitive information about a person or organization (the "mosaic effect"). What ethical and legal responsibilities does an analyst have when public data is lawful to access but could cause harm if combined or republished?

## Learning Resources

> **Ethics and scope note:** "Publicly available" is not the same as "free to use for any purpose." Records can contain personal information, and combining lawful, individually harmless records can expose sensitive details about a person (the mosaic effect). Use public records for legitimate, authorized purposes, respect the terms of each source, and be cautious about republishing aggregated personal data. In Canada, research relying solely on public information still has ethical limits that need professional review.

### Corporate and ownership records

*Supports: Research — attribution and link analysis; Reflect — organizational structure and supply chains*

- [SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/search-filings) — the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission's database of filings by public companies (10-K annual reports, 8-K current reports, insider Form 4s, and more). The [full-text search](https://efts.sec.gov/LATEST/search-index) covers filings from 2001 onward and is useful for finding every filer that mentions a person, company, or phrase.
- [OpenCorporates](https://opencorporates.com/) — the largest open database of companies, aggregating official registry data across many jurisdictions. Strong for identity and officer links; note that it does not publish beneficial-ownership data and that free access is limited for some uses.
- [Open Ownership Register](https://register.openownership.org/) — beneficial-ownership data (who ultimately owns or controls a company) standardized across several national registers. Directly relevant to supply-chain and ownership-structure questions.
- [GLEIF (Global Legal Entity Identifier Foundation)](https://search.gleif.org/) — a free search for Legal Entity Identifiers (LEIs), the global, standardized IDs for legal entities, plus "who owns whom" relationship data. Useful for reliably matching the same entity across datasets.

### Court and legal records

*Supports: Research — attribution; Investigate — finding the right database*

- [Decisions - Ontario Court of Justice](https://www.ontariocourts.ca/ocj/decisions/)
- [CourtListener / RECAP (Free Law Project)](https://www.courtlistener.com/recap/) — a free, searchable archive of millions of U.S. federal court documents and dockets gathered from PACER, which otherwise charges per page. Good for case filings, opinions, and party/judge searches.
- [PACER: Public Access to Court Electronic Records](https://pacer.uscourts.gov/) — the official U.S. federal court records system; the authoritative source when a document isn't already in RECAP (fees apply, with some free access and researcher fee exemptions).

### Open government and investigative data platforms

*Supports: Investigate — identifying relevant databases and extracting information*

- [Data.gov](https://data.gov/) — the U.S. federal open-data catalog, mandated by the OPEN Government Data Act, aggregating federal and many state and local datasets.
- [Open Government Portal (Canada)](https://open.canada.ca/en/open-data) — the Canadian federal equivalent, useful if the target or context is Canadian.
- [OCCRP Aleph](https://aleph.occrp.org/) — an investigative platform that lets you search across hundreds of datasets and document collections (company registries, gazettes, procurement, sanctions, court records) in one place. OCCRP notes that findings must be independently verified and that Aleph is not a standalone KYC solution.
- [ICIJ Offshore Leaks Database](https://offshoreleaks.icij.org/) — a searchable database of offshore entities from major leaks (Panama Papers and others), for ownership and affiliation research. Treat it as a lead source to corroborate, not proof on its own.

### Validation, cross-referencing, and ethics

*Supports: Research — validating and cross-referencing; Reflect — unintended exposure and the mosaic effect*

- [Verification Handbook for Investigative Reporting (Ch. 5: Verifying Data Quality)](https://datajournalism.com/read/handbook/verification-3) — Giannina Segnini's practical method for checking whether a dataset is complete and accurate before relying on it. Its core warning ("data lies") and its two-stage verification (on receipt, and again after analysis) directly answer the validation skill item.
- [GIJN: Researching Corporations and Their Owners](https://gijn.org/resource/researching-corporations-and-their-owners/) — a curated guide to official and unofficial company-data sources and how to use them together, including the limits of leaked and aggregated databases.
- [Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada: PIPEDA in brief](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-laws-in-canada/the-personal-information-protection-and-electronic-documents-act-pipeda/pipeda_brief/) — accessible framing for why personal information in public records remains sensitive and subject to privacy expectations.
- [TCPS 2 (2022), Article 2.2](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/policy-politique_tcps2-eptc2_2022.html) — the Canadian research-ethics rule that research relying solely on publicly available information is exempt from ethics review only when there is no reasonable expectation of privacy, a useful prompt for the mosaic-effect discussion.
